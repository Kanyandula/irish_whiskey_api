---
title: A Short History of Irish Whiskey
summary: From uisce beatha to near-extinction and back - how Irish whiskey lost the world and won it again.
---

Irish whiskey has the strangest arc of any major spirit: it invented the
category, dominated it globally, then very nearly disappeared altogether.

## Uisce beatha

The word itself is Irish. *Uisce beatha* - "water of life" - was anglicised to
"whiskey", and the practice of distilling in Ireland is documented from at
least the sixteenth century, with claims of monastic distilling running
considerably earlier. A licence to distil at Bushmills in County Antrim dates
to 1608, which is where the distillery's claim to be the world's oldest
licensed distillery comes from.

## The pot still and the malt tax

The defining Irish style was shaped by tax. A 1785 levy on malted barley made
100% malt mashes expensive, so Irish distillers mixed malted and unmalted
barley in the same pot still. What began as tax avoidance turned out to
produce something distinctive - the spicy, oily, creamy character now
protected as **single pot still Irish whiskey**, a style native to Ireland and
made nowhere else.

## Global dominance

Through the nineteenth century Irish whiskey was the most prestigious whiskey
in the world. Dublin's big four - Jameson, Powers, Roe, and George Roe's
neighbours in the Liberties - shipped globally, and Irish whiskey outsold
Scotch across the British Empire and the United States.

Then Aeneas Coffey, an Irishman, patented a continuous column still in 1830.
It made lighter, cheaper grain spirit at scale. Scottish distillers embraced
it and built the blended Scotch industry on it. The Dublin houses publicly
rejected it as producing an inferior product. They were arguably right on
quality and catastrophically wrong on commerce.

## Collapse

The twentieth century removed Irish whiskey's markets one at a time.
Prohibition closed the United States. The Anglo-Irish trade war of the 1930s
closed Britain and much of the Empire. Independence, two world wars, and the
loss of imperial trade routes did the rest. Distilleries that had been global
brands simply stopped.

By the 1960s the industry had contracted so far that the surviving southern
distillers - Jameson, Powers, and Cork Distilleries Company - merged into
**Irish Distillers** in 1966 to survive at all, consolidating production into
a single new site at Midleton, County Cork, in 1975. Bushmills joined in 1972.

At its lowest point, the entire island of Ireland had **two** working
distilleries.

## The turn

Cooley Distillery broke the monopoly in 1987, reviving dormant brands such as
Tyrconnell and Kilbeggan. But the real change came after 2010, when global
demand for premium whiskey met a generation of Irish founders willing to build
distilleries again - Dingle in 2012, Teeling returning distilling to Dublin in
2015 after a 125-year absence, Waterford in 2014, and dozens more.

Ireland went from a handful of working distilleries to over forty operational
sites in roughly fifteen years, and single pot still - the style the tax
accidentally created - is once again the category's calling card.
